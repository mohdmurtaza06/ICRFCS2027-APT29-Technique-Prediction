\begin{abstract}
Forecasting an adversary's next action is a useful goal for threat-informed defense, but ordered public attack traces are scarce. This paper reports a pilot benchmark of next-technique prediction on one ordered sequence reconstructed from the MITRE ATT\&CK Evaluations APT29 material. The five vendor result files yield identical ordered substep/technique pairs, but they describe the same emulation flow and are not independent campaigns. Candidates are drawn from an independent, version-pinned ATT\&CK Enterprise v18.1 catalogue of 835 technique IDs, not from the evaluation sequence. Models are trained on 500 heuristic tactic-constrained synthetic sequences per seed plus the first 98 observed steps, and are scored by teacher-forced top-$k$ ranking on the remaining 42 steps. Over three seeds, mean top-5 accuracy is 12.7\% for first-order Markov, 11.9\% for second-order Markov with backoff, 6.4\% for a local co-occurrence ranking, 5.6\% for an LSTM, and 1.6\% for global frequency; uniform random ranking would give 0.6\%. Three additional generator-policy checks show that scores also depend on tactic sampling and whether revoked/deprecated techniques are allowed in synthetic training: co-occurrence top-5 ranges from 3.2\% to 11.9\%, while LSTM top-5 ranges from 0\% to 5.6\% across tested conditions. These are descriptive pilot results on 42 sequentially dependent positions from one emulation; they do not establish cross-campaign or operational prediction capability. An earlier 58-label experiment whose scores cannot be reproduced is reported separately and is not comparable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
MITRE ATT\&CK provides a shared vocabulary for adversary tactics and techniques and is widely used in threat intelligence, detection engineering, and incident response \cite{roy2023sok}. A more ambitious task is predicting which technique may follow a partially observed sequence. Such predictions could help analysts prioritize investigation, but they are not deterministic forecasts: tactics express adversary goals, and operations can branch, repeat, omit, or reorder behaviors \cite{mitreTactics}.

Prior ATT\&CK research has studied technique associations and co-occurrence across reported groups and malware \cite{alshaer2020,rahman2026}. Association is not equivalent to temporal predictability, and research on telemetry mapping and report extraction addresses different tasks \cite{jiang2025,lupinacci2026,fayyazi2024,haque2026}. Execution-ordered public ground truth remains a practical constraint on directly evaluating next-step prediction.

This paper makes three limited contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item It defines next-technique prediction as a top-$k$ ranking task over an independent, versioned candidate universe (ATT\&CK Enterprise v18.1, 835 IDs), with fail-closed coverage checks so that no evaluation label can be added to the candidate set.
\item It reports a fully specified, deterministic, audited run of five ranking methods on a 98/42 chronological split of one APT29 sequence, with every ranked list released and every hit count recomputed independently.
\item It documents why the numbers cannot support cross-campaign or operational claims, including a quantified sensitivity to the synthetic generator's boundary rule.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{ATT\&CK Association and Sequence Modeling}
Al-Shaer \emph{et al.} studied statistical associations among techniques and used clustering to infer unobserved techniques from observed ones \cite{alshaer2020}; Erfan \emph{et al.} studied co-occurrence in adversary and malware technique data \cite{rahman2026}. Both motivate association-based baselines while leaving open whether ordered prefixes help predict the next technique.

A systematization of ATT\&CK research identifies application areas and validation gaps \cite{roy2023sok}; a later review covers 417 peer-reviewed publications \cite{jiang2025}. Mapping low-level telemetry to ATT\&CK \cite{lupinacci2026} and extracting techniques from reports with language models \cite{fayyazi2024,haque2026} are related but different from one-step-ahead forecasting.

Sequential threat forecasting predates technique-level work. Ghafir \emph{et al.} combine alert correlation with a hidden Markov model to predict APT stages \cite{ghafir2019}. Pappu studies smoothed higher-order Markov models for tactic prediction \cite{pappu2025}. Their prediction units and protocols differ from ours, so their metrics are not comparable.

Most directly, Raj \emph{et al.} introduce a hybrid LSTM--Markov framework for attack-chain forecasting using 4,849 campaign chains, and their publisher notes that the severity analysis has 33 independent campaign-level labels \cite{raj2026}. This paper therefore does not claim to be the first study of ATT\&CK sequence forecasting; its scope is a top-$k$ comparison on one APT29 evaluation sequence. Metrics are not comparable across the two studies, and no claim of advantage over the hybrid approach is made.

\subsection{Synthetic Attack Data and Emulation Sources}
APTGen generates artificial targeted-attack sequences from incident reports and executes corresponding activity \cite{takahashi2020}. The present generator is much simpler: a tactic-constrained random walk without report grounding or execution. The real sequence is reconstructed from public APT29 evaluation results \cite{mitreApt29,shen2024} and is a single evaluation artifact, not a sample of independent campaigns.

\section{Task and Evaluation Protocol}
Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the candidate universe, with $|\mathcal{T}|=V=835$ technique IDs read from the pinned ATT\&CK Enterprise v18.1 STIX bundle. Let the observed ordered sequence be $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$, $n=140$, with $s_i\in\mathcal{T}$. The first $c=98$ steps are initial context and the last $m=42$ are held out, $y_j=s_{c+j}$. At test position $j$, a method receives only $s_1,\ldots,s_{c+j-1}$ (earlier held-out steps are revealed after they occur: teacher forcing) and returns a ranked list $R_j^{(k)}$ of $k$ distinct candidates from $\mathcal{T}$. Top-$k$ accuracy is
\begin{equation}
 A_k=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{1}\{y_j\in R_j^{(k)}\}.
\end{equation}
The run aborts if any observed label is absent from $\mathcal{T}$; held-out labels are never appended to the candidate set. Ties are broken by ascending technique ID, and candidate lists are de-duplicated. A uniform random ranker has expected $A_k=k/835$, i.e., 0.12\%, 0.36\%, and 0.60\% for $k=1,3,5$.

Because the 58 observed labels are a small subset of $\mathcal{T}$, the task is much harder than the closed 58-label formulation of our earlier draft, and scores are not comparable with it. We pose three descriptive questions: (RQ1) do order-aware methods exceed order-agnostic ones on this split? (RQ2) does model complexity help? (RQ3) how much can be concluded from synthetic training data and a single real sequence? No population-level inference is attempted.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Real Sequence and Source Provenance}
The sequence is reconstructed from the public MITRE ATT\&CK Evaluations APT29 structured results, pinned to commit \texttt{8bee0d3} of the Joystick repository \cite{mitreApt29,mitreJoystick}. Flattening the technique-step records and sorting substeps by (major step, branch letter, numeric suborder) gives 140 instances with 140 unique substeps and 58 distinct technique IDs. The McAfee, Microsoft, FireEye, CrowdStrike, and Bitdefender files produced identical ordered (substep, technique) pairs, and each file's Git blob identifier matched the pinned value. This supports reproducibility of the ordering but does not create five samples: the files describe one MITRE-designed emulation flow, not a canonical chronology of all APT29 activity.

\subsection{Candidate Universe}
The universe contains every technique and sub-technique ID in the v18.1 Enterprise bundle (SHA-256 recorded in the released manifest): 835 IDs, of which 691 are active, 12 deprecated, and 132 revoked. Deprecated and revoked IDs are retained because the 2019 evaluation uses legacy identifiers. All 58 observed labels are present, but 23 of them are revoked or deprecated in v18.1 (13 of the 42 held-out positions have a revoked target), whereas such IDs are only 17.2\% of $\mathcal{T}$. All 58 observed labels are present in the pinned catalogue.

\subsection{Models}
We compare five ranking methods: global frequency, local co-occurrence association ranking, first-order Markov, second-order Markov with backoff, and an LSTM. The methods are rankers over the same catalogue, not closed-set classifiers.

\subsection{Synthetic Training Data}
Each seed generates 500 heuristic synthetic sequences with length sampled from 20 to 140, following a tactic-constrained walk over the catalogue. These synthetic sequences are an experimental convenience, not a validated model of real adversary behavior.

\subsection{Scoring and Reconciliation}
Every method returns a full, de-duplicated ranking over the same 835 IDs. Hits are scored at each of the 42 held-out positions with teacher forcing. Independent reconciliation code verifies the split, candidate validity, and reported counts; seed variation and sensitivity to synthetic generator choices are reported descriptively.

\section{Results}
In the primary condition, mean top-5 accuracy is 12.7\% for first-order Markov, 11.9\% for second-order Markov with backoff, 6.4\% for co-occurrence, 5.6\% for LSTM, and 1.6\% for global frequency. A uniform random ranker has expected top-5 accuracy of 0.6\%. The conditions are evaluated on the same dependent positions, so the means are descriptive comparisons, not independent replications.

The sensitivity runs show that synthetic generator design materially affects some results. Across tested conditions, co-occurrence top-5 accuracy ranges from 3.2\% to 11.9\%, while LSTM ranges from 0\% to 5.6\%. The benchmark therefore demonstrates sensitivity to assumptions rather than a stable model ordering.

\section{Limitations}
The study uses one emulation sequence, only 42 held-out positions, and teacher-forced context. Repeated positions are sequentially dependent, not independent samples. The tactic-constrained generator is heuristic, does not model attacker adaptation, and has no demonstrated fidelity to real-world operations. Performance on this benchmark cannot be interpreted as operational predictive accuracy, generalization across campaigns, or evidence of improved defensive outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
This pilot defines a reproducible open-catalogue next-technique ranking task and compares five baselines on a reconstructed APT29 evaluation sequence. Markov baselines perform best in the primary generator condition, but model ordering changes under sensitivity conditions. The evidence supports a narrow methodological conclusion: results depend strongly on task formulation and synthetic-data assumptions. Larger independent campaign collections and externally validated sequence generators are needed before stronger claims are warranted.
